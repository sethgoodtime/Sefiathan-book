# **Day 227 — August 16, 2026**

Sefiathan woke on his sixteenth morning at the waystation and the reading's declaration hit him before he'd fully opened his eyes: You can't be everything for everyone. Not gentle reminder. Absolute fact. That weight on your shoulders isn't the world—it's expectations you've collected like souvenirs. The words settled in his chest and he lay still, feeling how many expectations he'd been carrying. How many versions of himself he'd been trying to maintain simultaneously. How exhausting it had been to be everything for everyone when he could barely figure out what being himself actually meant.

He'd spent the past six days practicing saying no. Practicing honest preference. Practicing letting the mask of niceness crack. But the reading named something deeper now—the reason the mask had existed in the first place. He'd been trying to be everything for everyone. Had collected their expectations like souvenirs and carried them as proof he mattered. As evidence he was useful. As protection against being discarded if he stopped being whatever each person needed him to be.

Drop one today. Watch how quickly someone else picks it up. The catastrophe you fear won't happen. But your breath might return. Sefiathan sat up in bed and felt that land like permission he'd been waiting his whole life to receive. He could drop an expectation. Could stop trying to be what someone needed. Could let someone else pick up the responsibility he'd been convinced was his alone to carry. And the catastrophe he feared—that everything would collapse, that people would be disappointed, that he'd be revealed as inadequate—that wouldn't happen. But his breath might return.

The waystation was quiet around him and he realized he'd been holding his breath for sixteen days. For ten days trying to be the perfect grateful guest. For six days trying to be the perfect honest boundary-setter. Still performing. Still trying to be everything. Still collecting expectations like souvenirs even while learning to say no. The no's had been practice but they hadn't touched the deeper pattern—the belief that his worth depended on carrying everyone's expectations without dropping any.

Morning light filtered through the window and Sefiathan got dressed slowly, feeling the weight of what he'd been carrying. His mother's expectation that he be interested in her plants. His father's expectation that he be useful in practical ways. Elara's expectation that he be the easy sibling. The travelers' expectations that he be helpful and accommodating. Erenne's expectation—no, not Erenne's. She'd never asked him to be anything. The expectation he'd invented that he needed to be the perfect student, the ideal guest, the grateful recipient of her wisdom.

That was the souvenir he needed to drop today. The invented expectation that staying here meant being everything Erenne might need. Being useful in the garden. Being present for every meal. Being the student who absorbed every lesson. Being the companion who filled every silence. Being everything except actually himself.

Breakfast smells drifted from the kitchen and Sefiathan felt his automatic response rising—the pull to go help, to be present, to meet the expectation he'd invented. But he stopped. Felt the weight of it. Felt how carrying that invented expectation had been making him hold his breath for sixteen days. Felt how dropping it might let him breathe again.

He stayed in his room. Sat by the window watching grasslands wake in morning light. Didn't go help with breakfast. Didn't perform presence. Just sat. Just breathed. Just let the expectation drop and waited to see if the catastrophe would happen.

It didn't.

The waystation continued its morning rhythms without him. Erenne made breakfast. Travelers arrived and were greeted. The garden existed whether he tended it or not. Watch how quickly someone else picks it up. The work he'd been convinced was his responsibility simply happened without him. The expectation he'd been carrying as proof of his worth turned out to be completely unnecessary.

Midmorning brought Erenne to his door. "You okay?"

"Yeah. Just dropping an expectation I collected. Watching to see if everything collapses."

She smiled slightly. "And?"

"Nothing's collapsing. You made breakfast without me. The garden's fine. The waystation runs whether I'm being everything for it or not."

"Good. Took you sixteen days to figure that out."

The bluntness landed kindly. Of course it had taken him sixteen days. Of course he'd spent those days trying to be everything—first the perfect grateful guest, then the perfect honest boundary-setter, always performing some version of himself that would justify his presence. Always collecting expectations like souvenirs and carrying them as proof he deserved to stay.

The Don't list made perfect sense now. Lurking—he'd been lurking at the edges of his own life, trying to be invisible while simultaneously being everything everyone needed. Deleting numbers—trying to erase evidence of his own needs and preferences to make room for everyone else's. Eating your words—swallowing what he actually wanted to say in favor of what would meet expectations. All the ways he'd been trying to be everything for everyone instead of just being himself.

"What would it look like to not be everything?" Sefiathan asked Erenne later when they sat together on the porch. Not performing presence. Just actual companionship.

"Probably looks like breathing. Like taking up exactly the space you need instead of trying to fill everyone else's space too. Like letting other people be responsible for their own expectations instead of collecting them as your job."

"That sounds terrifying."

"Yeah. Because then you have to figure out what you actually are when you're not being everything for everyone. And you've been avoiding that question your whole life."

Truth landed clean and sharp. He had been avoiding that question. Had filled the space where that question lived with everyone else's expectations. Had made himself so busy being everything for everyone that he never had to discover what being himself actually meant. The expectations had been protection against that deeper uncertainty. If he was carrying everyone's needs, meeting everyone's preferences, being everything they required—then he didn't have to face the terrifying possibility that underneath all that performance, he might not know who he was at all.

But your breath might return. The reading's promise settled like benediction. If he dropped the expectations—if he stopped trying to be everything—his breath might return. The constant holding, the perpetual tension, the exhausting performance of being whatever each person needed—all of that could stop. And in the space it left, he might discover how to actually breathe.

Afternoon heat built and Sefiathan walked alone into grasslands, practicing dropping expectations with each step. Dropped the expectation that he needed to be useful to justify existing. Dropped the expectation that he needed to fill every silence. Dropped the expectation that his worth depended on meeting needs he'd invented for himself. Each dropped expectation felt like weight lifting. Like space opening. Like breath returning.

The Do list felt significant now. Sunglasses—protection that let him see clearly without being blinded. Candlelight—gentle illumination instead of harsh exposure. Finger food—permission to be casual, to not perform elaborate competence, to just eat simply without apology. All permission to be less than everything. To be simple. To be small. To be just himself without the weight of collected expectations.

Evening brought him back to the waystation and he found Erenne in the garden. Joined her not because he was supposed to but because he wanted to. Worked at his own pace without trying to match hers or meet some invented standard of helpfulness. Just worked. Just was. Just existed without trying to be everything.

"Better," Erenne said simply.

That night Sefiathan lay in bed feeling the catastrophe's absence. He'd dropped expectations all day and nothing had collapsed. The world had continued. People had adjusted. The work had happened without him carrying it. And in the space the dropped expectations left, his breath had started to return. Not fully yet. But beginning. Tentative. Testing whether it was safe to actually breathe instead of constantly holding.

You can't be everything for everyone. Simple truth but revolutionary for someone who'd spent twenty-seven years collecting expectations like souvenirs. Who'd carried them as proof of worth. Who'd believed dropping any would mean catastrophe. But the catastrophe hadn't happened. And his breath was returning. And tomorrow he'd practice dropping more. Practice being less than everything. Practice discovering what remained when he stopped trying to be what everyone needed and started just being himself—whoever that turned out to be.
