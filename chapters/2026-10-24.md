# **Day 296 — October 24, 2026**

Sefiathan woke on his twenty-fourth morning of October and the reading landed like permission to stop catastrophizing: Don't prepare for the worst until you know for certain. Your mind is creating elaborate disasters to solve problems that don't exist yet. Save that dramatic energy for actual crises, not the ones you're inventing.

He lay in bed feeling those words settle with uncomfortable recognition that made his stomach unclench slightly. The past four days since the reading about sharp wit and intellectual excitement had been strange—not difficult exactly, but disorienting in a different way than the homecoming revelation had been. He'd been thinking about what it would mean to find someone whose mind matched his speed, someone who found his tangential leaps delightful instead of exhausting, someone whose sharp wit would light up his brain the way the reading had described. And his mind had immediately started spinning elaborate scenarios about all the ways that could go wrong.

What if he found someone like that and they didn't find him interesting back? What if his sharpness was only exciting to him and actually annoying to everyone else? What if the person who could keep up with his thoughts existed but lived somewhere he'd never travel, or had already found someone else, or wouldn't be attracted to him physically even if they matched intellectually? What if he spent years looking for sharp wit and never found it and ended up alone because he'd set his standards too high? What if sharp wit plus sharp cheekbones didn't exist in the same person and he had to choose between intellectual excitement and physical attraction and made the wrong choice?

His mind had been rehearsing all of these disasters with increasing elaboration. Creating whole scenarios about conversations that would never happen, rejections he hadn't experienced, failures that existed only in his imagination. Stop rehearsing conversations that may never happen. Your mind is creating elaborate disasters to solve problems that don't exist yet. He'd been doing exactly that. Taking the permission to want intellectual excitement and immediately turning it into catastrophe.

Morning light filtered through his window and Sefiathan sat up slowly, feeling how exhausting the past few days had been. Not because anything had actually gone wrong. Because he'd been inventing problems and then dramatically solving them in his head over and over. Preparing for worst-case scenarios that weren't even real yet. Rehearsing rejections that hadn't happened. Building elaborate defenses against disasters he was creating himself.

Downstairs Erenne was making breakfast and when Sefiathan entered the kitchen she took one look at his face and laughed gently.

"You look like someone who's been catastrophizing again."

"The reading said I need to stop preparing for the worst until I know for certain. That my mind is creating elaborate disasters to solve problems that don't exist yet." He poured himself tea with hands that felt less tense than they had in days. "That I should save my dramatic energy for actual crises, not ones I'm inventing."

Erenne set down her spoon and looked at him directly. "What disasters have you been inventing this time?"

"All the ways finding someone with sharp wit could go wrong. What if they don't like me back. What if I'm only sharp to myself and actually boring to everyone else. What if they live somewhere I'll never go. What if sharp wit and attraction don't exist in the same person and I have to choose." He stopped, feeling how exhausting it was just listing them. "I've been rehearsing every possible rejection and failure before I've even met anyone."

"Your mind is creating elaborate disasters to solve problems that don't exist yet. You don't know that any of those things will happen. You're just preparing for them as if they're inevitable."

They ate together and Sefiathan felt the reading working through him. Don't prepare for the worst until you know for certain. How much energy had he spent over the years preparing for disasters that never happened? Rehearsing difficult conversations that never occurred? Building defenses against rejections he never experienced? His mind was good at catastrophizing. Skilled at taking any possibility and spinning it into worst-case scenario. But that skill wasn't actually protecting him. Was just exhausting him with imaginary crises.

The Do list felt deliberately grounding. Seltzer, Snack bowl, Encyclopedias. Physical comfort in your actual body instead of mental gymnastics. Snack bowl—tend to real present needs instead of imaginary future disasters. Encyclopedias—ground yourself in what's actually true and verifiable instead of speculation. All of it pointing toward the same thing. Save that dramatic energy for actual crises, not the ones you're inventing.

"I think I've been treating catastrophizing like preparation," he said quietly. "Like if I imagine all the worst possibilities ahead of time, I'll be ready for them. But I'm not actually getting ready. I'm just—exhausting myself with disasters that don't exist."

"And even if some of them do happen eventually, rehearsing them now doesn't make them easier. Just makes you live through them multiple times instead of once."

Morning stretched into midday and Sefiathan walked out into the grasslands feeling this permission settling through him like unfamiliar lightness. Stop preparing for the worst until you know for certain. He found his spot by the creek and sat down, and immediately his mind started generating new disasters. What if the journey never gave him clarity about where he belonged? What if he reached the end of the year without understanding what it had all been for? What if—

But he caught himself. Those were invented problems. Elaborate disasters for situations that hadn't happened yet. He didn't know whether the journey would give him clarity. Didn't know what the end of the year would look like. Didn't know any of it for certain. So why was he preparing for the worst as if it were inevitable?

The Don't list felt pointed. Permanent marker, Hangups, Hit refresh. Don't mark these catastrophes as permanent truth—they're speculation. Don't get hung up on disasters you're inventing. Don't keep refreshing your anxiety by generating new worst-case scenarios. The reading was giving permission to let the future be unknown without filling the uncertainty with catastrophe.

He sat by the creek and practiced not preparing. Not rehearsing. Not building elaborate defenses against disasters that didn't exist yet. Just sitting with not knowing. With uncertainty. With the reality that he couldn't predict how finding sharp wit would go, or whether the journey would give him answers, or what would happen when the year ended. And that not knowing didn't require him to prepare for the worst. Could just be—not knowing.

It felt strange. His mind kept wanting to grab onto scenarios and spin them into crisis. Kept wanting to rehearse conversations and prepare responses and build defenses. But every time he noticed it happening he came back to the reading. Your mind is creating elaborate disasters to solve problems that don't exist yet. Was it a real problem or an invented one? Did he know for certain or was he catastrophizing? Was this actual crisis or dramatic energy spent on imagination?

Afternoon brought Korrin to the creek and when he sat down Sefiathan turned to him with unusual directness.

"Do you catastrophize?"

Korrin blinked. "Sometimes. Why?"

"The reading said I need to stop preparing for the worst until I know for certain. That my mind creates elaborate disasters to solve problems that don't exist yet. And I've been realizing I do that constantly. Take any uncertainty and immediately spin it into worst-case scenario."

"Like what?"

"Like with the sharp wit thing. The reading a few days ago said to look for someone whose mind excites mine. And instead of just—wanting that and being open to it, I immediately started catastrophizing. What if they don't like me back. What if I never find them. What if sharp wit and attraction don't exist together. I invented about seventeen different disasters before I'd even met anyone."

Korrin smiled. "Seventeen seems conservative for you."

"I'm serious. I rehearse conversations that will never happen. Prepare for rejections I haven't experienced. Build elaborate defenses against problems I'm inventing. And it's exhausting."

"So stop."

"But what if I'm not prepared when bad things actually happen?"

"You'll handle them when they happen. If they happen. Rehearsing them now doesn't make them easier—just makes you live through them multiple times." Korrin looked at him carefully. "Save that dramatic energy for actual crises, not the ones you're inventing."

They sat together and Sefiathan felt something fundamental shifting in how he held uncertainty. His mind was dramatic. Good at spinning scenarios. Skilled at catastrophizing. But that skill wasn't serving him. Wasn't actually preparing him for anything. Was just exhausting him with imaginary disasters while the actual present moment went by unrehearsed. Don't prepare for the worst until you know for certain. He didn't know for certain. Didn't know how anything would unfold. And that was okay. Could just be uncertain without filling the uncertainty with catastrophe.

Evening brought them back to the waystation where Erenne had dinner ready. And when she asked about his day, Sefiathan answered with less dramatic energy than usual.

"I've been catastrophizing. Taking every uncertainty and spinning it into worst-case scenario. Rehearsing disasters that don't exist yet. But the reading said to stop preparing for the worst until I know for certain. To save my dramatic energy for actual crises."

"And how does it feel to stop catastrophizing?"

"Lighter. Strange. My mind keeps wanting to grab onto scenarios and spin them into crisis. But when I catch it happening and come back to what's actually real—it's just quieter."

Later in his room Sefiathan lay in bed feeling October moving forward. Twenty-four days into this month and learning about walls and lies and sharpness and now about catastrophes. About recognizing his mind was skilled at creating elaborate disasters to solve problems that didn't exist yet. About understanding that preparing for worst-case scenarios wasn't actually protective, just exhausting. About permission to let the future be uncertain without filling it with catastrophe. About saving his dramatic energy for actual crises instead of invented ones.

Don't prepare for the worst until you know for certain. He wouldn't. Starting now. No more rehearsing conversations that might never happen. No more building defenses against disasters he was inventing. No more spinning every uncertainty into worst-case scenario. Your mind is creating elaborate disasters to solve problems that don't exist yet. It was. But it didn't have to. He could just—not know. Could be uncertain without catastrophizing. Could wait to see what actually happened instead of preparing for every possible worst outcome. Save that dramatic energy for actual crises. Tomorrow would bring what it brought. Maybe clarity. Maybe more uncertainty. Maybe sharp wit or continued searching or something he couldn't predict. But he didn't need to rehearse it all ahead of time. Didn't need to prepare for the worst. Could just meet whatever came when it actually arrived instead of inventing it early and living through it multiple times in his mind.
